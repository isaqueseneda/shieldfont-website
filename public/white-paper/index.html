WHITE PAPER · CONCEAL · PASS THE GRADER · POISON ↔ STALE · SHIPPING MAPPING v18

ShieldFont is a sleight of hand. The HTML source carries swapped words; the font renders them back to the originals on screen. A person sees the sentence you wrote. A scraper copying the raw HTML sees a different one. This paper reports what that buys and what it does not: it measures the meaning a reader loses straight from the text, it prices what a scraper would have to spend to undo it, and it is open that a scraper willing to read the font can undo it cheaply. Every measurement here is on the shipping dictionary, v18-alpha.

§ 01ABSTRACTTWO PARAGRAPHS

ShieldFont swaps common English words for unusual same-part-of-speech ones, so a scraper reading the raw HTML ingests scrambled text while a human sees the original through the font. A shielded page has to clear three bars at once: conceal the meaning, get past the grader that decides which pages reach training, and be worth nothing to whatever trains on it. This paper measures the first two directly. Swapping 24.4% of running words, all of them content words, a public entailment classifier judges that 50.4% of passages no longer make the same factual claim as the original (pre-registered median, n=1,500 passages per corpus, CC-News / OpenWebText / PG-19), against 2.1% for a WordNet synonym-swap control run at the same swap density. That control is the load-bearing part: it is what separates measured meaning loss from a classifier being confused by unusual words. Then the second bar. Of the pages that would have cleared a real training pipeline's educational-quality grader before shielding, 9.70% still clear it after (13 of 134). Roughly nine in ten are thrown away, and about one in ten reaches training carrying words the author never wrote. Both headlines carry their caveats here rather than in a footnote: the entailment figure comes from a single 84-million-parameter classifier with no human raters, and the retention figure rests on a small denominator whose 95% confidence interval runs from 5.8% to 15.9%. Every number in this paper is measured on the shipping dictionary, v18-alpha.

We use three words for three different things and never let a number cross between them. Poison is a claim about what a model learns, and we define it narrowly (§ 06), cite the one controlled experiment that supports the mechanism, state the dose evidence that argues against us, and do not claim it. Stale is a claim about what a pipeline keeps, which is § 05. Empty calories, our phrase and not a term of art, is what a kept page costs: 24.1% of a surviving page's word budget carries no real meaning. And the load-bearing caveat, set here rather than buried: the font is invertible. The browser must be handed the font to render the page, so anyone who downloads it can read the substitution table out of it. We did exactly this against the shipped file: the composite glyphs are drawn from the original word's own letters, so joining them with the ligature table returns all 11,962 pairs, no dictionary and no guessing. An earlier version of this paper priced that attack at 43 seconds per site. That figure was wrong, and it was wrong in our favour. Thirty-nine of those seconds were our own Python font library unpacking a compressed file and under one second was the attack; measured properly a competent implementation costs under a second per site, or about five dollars of compute to invert a million shielded sites. What survives is a smaller, truer argument: the meaning loss you can measure straight from the text, an engineering cost of one to three engineer-weeks that somebody has to pay before any of this is free, and the fact that no published pretraining pipeline renders anything at all (§ 02.2). See § 04.1 for the entailment metric, § 05 for the grader, § 06 for what we will and will not claim about training damage, and § 10 for the measurement roadmap.

§ 01.1THE SLEIGHT OF HANDINTRODUCTION
— START HERE

You and a scraper are reading two different documents.

Every time you publish online, an AI scraper can copy your words to train its next model. ShieldFont hands it a decoy. Your readers see the sentence you wrote, “The knight rode his horse into battle”, while a scraper copying the page's source reads a different one: “The knight rode his engine into battle.” Same space on the page, two documents. Change about a quarter of the words, all of them content words, and the machine loses most of the meaning.

One swapped word and the fact is now false: the knight's mount is gone, and a model that trained on the source would learn the wrong thing with full confidence. The rest of this introduction covers why that swap is invisible to you, why one page is worth doing, and the three tests every shielded page has to pass.

§ 01.3THREE BARS, ALL AT ONCETHE MAP
— HOW TO READ THIS PAPER

Conceal. Pass the grader. Waste the compute.

Every shielded page has to clear three bars at the same time. Conceal: hide the meaning from the machine while a human reads the page normally. Pass the grader: stay grammatical and fluent enough that a training pipeline keeps the page instead of throwing it away as junk. Nothing on the open web goes straight into a model: every large pipeline scores pages first and discards most of them, and that scorer is what we call the grader. Reach training and be worth nothing when it does (empty calories, our phrase): if the grader keeps the page, make sure the words it carries teach the model something the author never wrote. The rest of the paper walks these three bars, strongest evidence first inside each one.

Two words carry most of the argument and we keep them apart on purpose. Stale is a claim about what a pipeline keeps and how little it is worth; we can measure that, and § 05 does. Poison is a claim about what a model learns; we define it narrowly in § 06, show the one controlled experiment that supports the mechanism, and do not claim it. They are not opposites. They are the two ends of one dial, and which end you land on depends on a design choice we explain in § 06.4.

And one honest limit, set here rather than buried. The font is the codebook, and the browser has to download it to render the page, so a scraper that bothers to read the font can invert it and recover the original words. We ran that attack on the shipped file: about forty lines of code returned the complete table, all 11,962 pairs with no errors, in under a second per site once you use a compiled font library rather than the Python one we first measured with (§ 02.2, § 10.1). Concealment does not stop a determined, technical adversary. What it does is make somebody build the tool first, and then charge them per site rather than per page. The bars that survive that fact are the meaning loss you can measure straight from the text and the staleness donated to any pipeline that does not stop to invert, which today is all of them.

§ 02HOW AI READS TEXTMETHODOLOGY
— CONTEXT

A model never sees the page. It sees the source.

When a scraper visits a shielded page, it pulls the raw HTML, never the rendered glyphs. The font's ligatures only resolve at paint time inside a real browser. So the model receives a paragraph where common content words have been quietly swapped for rarer ones, and it processes those substitutes at the token level as if they were what the author meant.

Two things can happen next. The model may still infer the meaning correctly, in which case concealment failed for that reader. Or it may absorb the substitutes as if they were the text, in which case the page is stale training signal: grammatical, but carrying the wrong associations. This paper measures how often each happens.

— WHO ACTUALLY READS THE PAGE

It is worth knowing how literally true “it sees the source” is, because the whole design depends on it. We checked every large public training pipeline we could find a method paper for. None of them draw the page. FineWeb and RefinedWeb pull text out of the raw crawl files with trafilatura; DCLM uses resiliparse, chosen explicitly because it is “8× faster… more practical for large-scale processing”; C4 uses the crawl's own pre-extracted text; The Pile uses jusText; Dolma uses CCNet. The closest anything gets to a browser is NVIDIA's maths corpus, which drives Lynx, a text-only browser from the 1990s that has never rendered a pixel in its life. The crawlers agree: a measurement of roughly 500 million fetches published by Vercel and MERJ found GPTBot, ClaudeBot and PerplexityBot pull raw HTML and run no JavaScript at all, though Google-Extended and AppleBot do render, which we name rather than hide. This is not a claim that rendering is impossible. It is a claim about what is deployed, at scale, today, and § 02.2 prices what changing it would cost.

§ 02.2THE THREAT MODELFOUR WAYS IN
— WHAT AN ADVERSARY CAN ACTUALLY DO

Four ways to get the words back, and what each one costs.

There are exactly four routes from a shielded page to the words the author wrote. Read the raw HTML and take the decoys. Download the font and invert it. Drive a real browser and read what it produces. Or draw the page as an image and read the picture. They differ by three orders of magnitude in price, and only one of them is what anybody does today. This section prices all four, in that order, and says plainly which ones defeat us.

Three terms, defined once. Scraping is downloading a page's source code. The DOM is the live document the browser builds out of that source, and it is what automation tools read when they “look at” a page. OCR, optical character recognition, is reading text back out of a picture of text. ShieldFont lives in the gap between the source and the picture: the swap is undone by the font at the moment of drawing, so the source carries decoys and only the drawn page carries the original.

— ROUTE A · PLAIN HTML

About $0.74 per million pages. Recovers the decoys. Everyone does it. This is the whole industry, for the reasons § 02 sets out. It is also the cheapest thing in this document: a published 2025 crawl of 1.005 billion pages cost $462, about $0.46 per million, and heuristic text extraction at 28 milliseconds of processor time per page adds roughly $0.28 more. Every other number in this section is a multiple of that $0.74. A shielded page handed to this route yields decoys, in full, with no way to tell they are decoys.

— ROUTE B · FONT INVERSION

About $5 in compute per million sites, plus one to three engineer-weeks, once. Recovers every word. Nobody does it publicly, yet. This is the attack that beats us, and it has beaten us since the first version. The browser cannot draw a shielded page without the font, so the font ships to every visitor, and the font is the answer key. Each decoy word is drawn by a single composite glyph, which is a drawing assembled out of smaller drawings, and the smaller drawings it is assembled out of are the letters of the original word. Join those components against the font's own substitution table and you get decoy to original directly, with no dictionary, no guessing and no seed. We ran it against our shipped file and recovered 11,962 of 11,962 word pairs with zero errors. That part has not changed and will not change.

What has changed is the price, and the correction is ours to own. We first reported the attack at about 43 seconds per site and put that figure in the abstract. It was wrong, and it was wrong in our own favour. Measured properly on the same machine with the same code: reading the composite glyphs out of the uncompressed font takes 0.93 seconds; out of the compressed file we ship, 37.6 seconds, almost all of it our Python library rebuilding the font by hand. Decompressing the same file with the standard C tool takes 13.3 milliseconds. The 43 seconds was 39 seconds of our tooling and under one second of attack, which inflated the true figure roughly forty times.

The honest number is under one second per site. A competent implementation costs $0.01 to invert a thousand sites, $0.50 for a hundred thousand and $5.02 for a million; even the naive pure-Python version costs $402 per million. Compute is not the barrier. The barrier is that somebody has to build it: one to three engineer-weeks, call it $8,000 to $25,000, paid once and never again. For a frontier lab that is a rounding error on a Tuesday. For the tens of thousands of smaller scrapers and one-off pipelines that make up most of the traffic hitting a small publisher, it is a project nobody has scheduled. We are not claiming that gap is permanent. We are claiming it is the actual shape of the defence, and it is worth more than a fake 43 seconds.

— ROUTE C · HEADLESS RENDER

Roughly 2× to 8× Route A. Recovers the decoys from every text surface and the originals from one PDF surface. Google-Extended and AppleBot render; no pretraining corpus does. A headless browser is a real browser with no window on screen, driven by a script. The common assumption, including in an earlier draft of this paper, is that running one defeats ShieldFont because the browser “sees” the rendered text. It does not, and we checked twelve different ways. Against a controlled page in headless Chrome, every text surface the browser exposes returned the decoy: the DOM dump, outerHTML, textContent, innerText, the text selection a user would copy to the clipboard, the accessibility tree that Playwright and Puppeteer expose as accessibility.snapshot(), the DOM snapshot, the saved-page archive, and printing to PDF followed by ordinary text extraction. The one call that reports anything about fonts returns a count of glyphs and no identities. A complete dump of the browser automation protocol, all 58 command families including the experimental ones, contains the word “glyph” exactly three times, all three inside that one count.

This is not luck, it is how text shaping works. The substitution happens during layout, the shaper keeps input characters and output drawings in separate buffers, and nothing ever writes the drawing back into the text. So rendering gives them the decoy, at two to eight times the price of not rendering. Two honest exceptions, both ours to declare. One is a real defeat. Chrome's print-to-PDF embeds a subset of the font in the output file, 25,892 drawings' worth. Take those apart the same way Route B does and the original words fall out: on a nine-word test we recovered five exactly and the rest were trivially completable from any dictionary. That is a headless-only break with no picture drawn at all, so anyone already producing PDFs gets a partial inversion for free. The second is not a security property. The accessibility tree returns the decoy verbatim, which means a screen reader voices gibberish. We own that, and § 12 keeps it in the limitations list.

There is also a narrow side channel worth stating so nobody discovers it and calls it a finding. Measuring the on-screen width of individual characters inside a shielded word returns suspiciously uniform values (17.25, 17.23, 17.25, 17.23) where ordinary text varies (16.64, 15.75, 15.70). That reveals which words are shielded, not what they say. It is a detection oracle, not a decoding one, and § 02.5 explains why detection is the thing we care about most.

— ROUTE D · RENDER AND OCR

Roughly 200× Route A self-hosted, roughly 800× at cloud volume rates. Recovers everything, perfectly. Nobody does it at pretraining scale. This is the route that works, and we tell people about it first, because pretending otherwise would be embarrassing. Render the page, take a picture, read the picture. Off-the-shelf Tesseract returned the original text at 100% word accuracy and a 0.00% character error rate. ShieldFont is not un-readable by machine and has never claimed to be. It is simply, per page, two to three orders of magnitude more expensive than reading the HTML, which § 02.3 prices out.

One piece of prior work belongs here rather than in § 11.1, because a reviewer will otherwise land it on us. Boucher, Shumailov, Anderson and Papernot's “Bad Characters” (IEEE Security and Privacy, 2022) explicitly recommends render-and-read as the catch-all defence against text-encoding obfuscation, measured as fully effective at a 6.2% cost in translation quality. They are right, and theirs is the correct answer to the question they asked, which was how one system should defend one input it already suspects. Our threat model is the other one: a pipeline deciding, ahead of time and for every page on the open web, whether that page is worth the 200×. § 02.3 is that arithmetic and § 02.5 is why they cannot narrow the set.

§ 02.3THE ECONOMICSPER SITE VS PER PAGE
— ORDER OF MAGNITUDE, NOT PRECISION

Reading the web is nearly free. Looking at it is not.

The gap between the cheapest way to read the web and the cheapest way to look at it is the whole economic argument, so here it is in dollars rather than adjectives. Fetching three billion pages costs about $1,400, and about $2,200 once you have also extracted the text. Running text recognition over the same three billion pages costs $423,000 to $528,000 if you build the rig yourself and $1.22 million to $1.80 million if you buy it from a cloud vendor at volume rates. Same pages, same web. One is a hobby budget and the other is an institution's annual accounts.

The render step alone is the 8× that people quote. Four scraping vendors publish the multiplier for turning a plain fetch into a full browser render and they cluster tightly, from 5× to 12.7×. The physical reason is unglamorous: the median web page in 2025 is 22 KB of HTML inside 2,862 KB of total weight, so rendering means fetching roughly a hundred times the bytes you needed. Reading the picture on top is what takes it past 200×, and past 800× at cloud rates. That is the number worth carrying out of this paper. 8× is the part of the bill you can shrug at.

— THE FRAMING THAT LANDS HARDEST, AND THREE NUMBERS WE ARE RETIRING

Dollar totals are the weakest way to make this point, because $528,000 is a rounding error against the cost of a training run and any lab reading this knows it. The institutional version lands harder. Common Crawl's entire operating budget for 2024 was $1,326,638, and with it the foundation crawled 26.67 billion pages across ten crawls, with storage donated so that figure excludes roughly $2.65 million a year at list price. Suppose it decided to run text recognition over three billion of those pages, eleven percent of the year's output. At the cheapest benchmarked self-hosted rate it would consume 32% of the foundation's entire annual budget to look properly at 11% of its pages. That is the shape of the ask. Not that it is unaffordable, because for a frontier lab it plainly is affordable, but that it turns crawling from something a nonprofit runs on a million dollars a year into a line item competing with a training run, for a benefit nobody has shown is worth having.

Three numbers we are retiring while we are here. Earlier ShieldFont material put the cost of machine-reading the web in the billions; that has no source we can stand behind and the range above replaces it. The widely repeated claim that crawling the web costs about $1.5 million traces to no primary source we could find, so we do not cite it and suggest nobody else does. And we will not say self-hosted recognition is orders of magnitude cheaper than cloud: the cheapest published cloud rate is only about 2.9 times the cheapest benchmarked self-hosted one. The orders of magnitude here are between reading pictures and reading HTML, not between vendors.

Per site versus per page is the structure that actually decides this. Font inversion is priced per site, because one font decodes every page that font serves. Scraping is priced per page. Against a large publisher, inversion amortises to nothing: spread across ten thousand pages it adds 0.25% to the cost of scraping that site, and nobody is deterred by a quarter of one percent. Against the long tail the same structure inverts. Below roughly 25 pages per site, inverting a site's font costs more than reading the site did. A forty-page personal site, a photographer's portfolio, a small press, a researcher's page of essays: for those, inversion is the majority of the bill. And the long tail is not a niche. It is most of the web by site count, and it is precisely who ShieldFont was built for.

§ 02.4DEFENCES WE REJECTEDA NEGATIVE RESULT
— WE TRIED TO BEAT THE INVERSION ATTACK. WE COULD NOT.

Eight ways to make the font harder to invert. Six of them fail, and the two that work make it easier.

Once we knew the font was invertible, the obvious move was to make inverting it harder. We spent a research round on that, tried eight approaches including two proposed by this project's own founder, and built and measured the ones that could be built. Six fail. Two work, and neither of them is a defence. The two that work are performance improvements that shrink the font, which if anything makes inversion marginally cheaper, and we are shipping them anyway because they are good engineering.

We are publishing this because a negative result about our own proposals is worth more than a defence we cannot support. It also converges on a single conclusion that shapes the rest of the paper: stop trying to defeat inversion, and price it instead.

— THE FOUNDER'S TWO IDEAS, AND WHY BOTH FAIL

Split each glyph into a top half and a bottom half, so that even if an attacker pulls the drawings apart they cannot tell which letter is which. Good instinct, three failures. First, a composite drawing cannot crop: in this font format a composite is a list of pointers to other drawings plus an offset, with no clip and no mask, so “the top half of the letter a” has to become a genuinely new drawing with real outlines. If the halves are shared across words, which is what keeps the font small enough to ship, the attacker's alphabet grows from 52 letters to 104 half-letters: a bigger table to build once, in minutes, and the component list still reads out in coordinate order, so the letter sequence is still sitting in the file. If the halves are not shared, you have re-invented flattening and inherited its costs. Second, the machinery does not permit it: the rule that turns a decoy word into a single drawing outputs exactly one drawing by definition, and two visible halves would need the one-to-many rule, which is already spoken for performing the reversal at word boundaries. The idea collides with the mechanism it is trying to protect. Third, split glyphs break the shielded font in Word, Pages and InDesign, which is the whole download tier.

Draw the glyphs as SVG images instead, on the theory that a picture is harder to reason about than a data structure. The instinct is right and the implementation is backwards. The SVG table in a font stores plain-text markup with the path coordinates written out in the clear. It is not a picture, it is a text file describing one, and reading a word's letter shapes out of it is strictly easier than reading them out of the compact binary outlines we use now. This defence has a negative attacker cost. It fails twice more on top of that: the SVG table exists for colour glyphs and needs ordinary outlines as a fallback anyway, so you ship the key twice, and the substitution machinery operates on those outlines regardless. Word, Pages and InDesign do not support it either. The generalised version of the idea, serving text as images rather than text, is a different and legitimate proposal. It is also not ShieldFont: it destroys selectable text, search indexing and screen readers outright, and this project's premise is that the page stays a real page.

— THE TWO WE BUILT AND ATTACKED, AND THE TWO THAT WORK

Flattening, drawing every word as one continuous set of outlines with no letter structure left, fails on a detail of how the fonts are built: every component is placed with a plain whole-number shift and no rotation, scaling or skew, and all 270,747 placements in the shipped file are pure translations. So every flattened outline is a byte-for-byte copy of a base letter, just moved. Slide each back to the origin, hash it, look it up: 69 distinct shapes cover all 53 letters, the table builds in 0.01 seconds, and the attack recovers all 11,962 pairs exactly in 14.3 seconds. One afternoon of attacker engineering against 16% more bytes, four times the memory and a 68-second load for us. Jitter, nudging every point so no two copies of a letter match, is worse and fails for a reason better engineering cannot fix: the jitter has to be invisible, invisible means small, and small perturbation is exactly what shape matching is robust against. Proper clustering recovered 75.8% of words and 87.2% of characters in 10.8 seconds. Meanwhile compression works by noticing the same shape repeats thousands of times, jitter destroys that, and the font goes from about 1 MB to 12.3 MB. For the record, this is the only way we have found to actually produce a 12 MB ShieldFont file, and it would be entirely self-inflicted.

Drop the glyph-name table. Fonts carry human-readable names for every drawing; on the web that table has no rendering function at all, and dropping it makes the shipped font 18% smaller, from 1,007,896 bytes to 826,332. This is done. The web, CDN and React fonts now ship with no glyph names in them at all, verified as a complete round-trip against the encoder, which deletes that attack surface outright rather than obscuring it and makes hashing or salting those names moot for everything served over the web. The downloadable desktop file keeps its names, because desktop applications expect them. Ship only the words a site actually uses. The default font carries the full 12,011-word codebook because it must work on any text; a given site does not use twelve thousand distinct swappable words. Pruning the substitution tables first, then subsetting, gets a site using two thousand distinct words down to 196,884 bytes instead of 1,006,652, 5.1 times smaller. The naive approach does not work, because the substitution rules pull every word back in, so this is measured rather than shipped by default. Neither is a defence: both make the font smaller, faster to download and faster to parse, which makes inversion marginally cheaper. We are shipping them anyway, and saying so out loud, because a paper that shipped a slower font to make an attack look more expensive would deserve everything it got.

The eight results converge on one sentence. You cannot move a key you must hand to the reader. The browser has to have the font, the font has to draw the original letters or the page does not render, and every scheme for hiding the letters inside the drawing either fails to hide them, breaks the rendering, or costs more than it protects. So the paper stops claiming concealment against a determined technical adversary, and claims three things it can support instead: the meaning loss you can measure straight from the text (§ 04.1), the staleness donated to every pipeline that does not stop to invert, which as § 02.2 shows is currently all of them, and the identification problem in § 02.5 that decides whether the expensive routes are ever affordable.

§ 02.5COLLECTIVE DEFENCETHE IDENTIFICATION PROBLEM
— THE ASSUMPTION EVERY COST IN § 02.3 DEPENDS ON

Every expensive attack assumes they already know which pages to attack.

Go back through § 02.3 and notice what every affordable number quietly assumes. Inverting a million sites for five dollars assumes you know which million sites. OCR at 200 times the price of HTML is only survivable if you run it on a small identified subset. Take away the ability to tell a shielded page from an ordinary one and every cost in this paper has to be paid on every page on the web. § 01.2 put this as an orchard: the thief cannot see which oranges are bitter, so they must distrust the tray or pay to check every fruit. This section is the same argument with the receipts attached, and with an honest account of where it holds and where it does not.

Where it does not hold: inversion. If an adversary has built the tool, running it on every site rather than the shielded ones costs five dollars per million sites instead of five dollars per million shielded sites. Unidentifiability does not make inversion expensive, and anyone who says otherwise has not done the arithmetic. What it does is narrower and still real: it removes the cheap option of skipping shielded pages and forces the tool to handle the general case rather than one known font, which is engineering cost, the only cost that gates this route at all.

Where it holds completely: reading pictures. That route is affordable exactly when you can point it at a small identified set, and unaffordable when you cannot, which is why the Common Crawl arithmetic in § 02.3 is written as 32% of the annual budget for 11% of the pages rather than as a total. If you cannot tell which pages are shielded, the only sound policies are to read every picture, invert every font, or accept the staleness. Two of those are institutionally implausible and the third is what this project is arguing for. And where it decides everything: the ordinary skip. The cheapest countermeasure against ShieldFont is not inversion or recognition, it is a filter rule: detect the tell, drop the page, move on. That costs approximately nothing and defeats us completely on that page, which is the honest reason detection matters more than decoding. § 02.2 named one real tell, the uniform character widths, and any fixed class name, font name or boilerplate note is another. This is why the camouflage in the shipped implementation is not decoration: the public font files carry neutral names, the CSS class is neutral, the markup carries a generic attribute rather than a brand, and nothing in the bytes a visitor downloads says ShieldFont. But an implementation detail on one project cannot carry the argument, because any fixed tell is eventually a fingerprint. Which is where this stops being an engineering problem and becomes a collective one.

— THREE THINGS MAKE IDENTIFICATION HARD, AND ONLY ONE IS OURS TO BUILD

Different dictionaries. Every site that mints its own produces a font that decodes only itself. There is no single precomputed table that unlocks the web, so the attacker pays per target rather than once. Decoy markers. A page that carries every visible sign of being shielded, the class names, the font, the attributes, while its text is completely ordinary, costs its publisher nothing and costs the adversary a full inversion or a full OCR pass to discover it wasted the money. Enough of those and detection stops being a filter and becomes a tax. This is the cheapest contribution any site can make and it does not require encoding a word. Other techniques entirely. ShieldFont is one way to make the source disagree with the page. It is not the only one and it should not be. The more independent techniques are in circulation, the less any single detector is worth, and the closer the adversary gets to the only robust policy, which is to treat all text as suspect.

Put plainly: the goal is not to make one page unreadable. It is to make the whole scrape untrustworthy. A thousand sites each shielding differently, plus an unknown number of decoy sites shielding nothing at all, is not a filtering problem with a clean solution. It is the orchard.

We should be exact about the size of the claim, because the temptation to inflate it is enormous. ShieldFont does not stop AI training. It does not stop a determined adversary reading one specific site. It does not survive contact with someone who has spent three engineer-weeks and decided your site is worth it. What it does is put sand in a machine that currently runs frictionless. It makes an adversary choose between paying per target, paying 200 times the price to look at pictures, or taking the text and accepting that some fraction of it means nothing. That is a real choice where currently there is none, and buying time is a legitimate objective when the alternative is unconsented extraction with no cost attached at all.

§ 04.1MEANING DAMAGE · ENTAILMENTPRE-REGISTERED · n=1,500 PER CORPUS
— THE HERO CLAIM

Swap 24.4% of words. Break the claim in 50.4% of passages.

Counting swapped words, counting how rare the substitutes are, measuring how far an embedding moved: all of these are proxies for the thing that actually matters, which is whether the shielded text still means what the original meant. Swap-counting is a poor unit for that. So we measured meaning survival as directly as one classifier allows. An entailment failure is close to a plain count: of N pairs of passages, M no longer make the same claim. It is a reading, not another proxy estimate.

— WHAT WE MEASURED, FROM SCRATCH

Entailment is the plain question: if the first piece of text is true, does the second one follow from it? It is the standard test the field uses for whether two pieces of writing are making the same claim, and there are off-the-shelf classifiers that answer it. Given a pair of texts, such a classifier returns three numbers: how likely the second follows from the first, how likely it is unrelated, and how likely the two actively contradict each other.

We asked it in both directions on every pair of an original passage and its shielded version: first whether the shielded text follows from the original, then whether the original follows from the shielded text. A pair fails both-ways entailment if either direction scores below a half, meaning the classifier no longer thinks the two texts make the same claim. A pair shows a strong contradiction if either direction's contradiction score goes above a half, meaning the classifier thinks the two texts are incompatible.

Asking it both ways is the right test because content-word swaps do far more damage than their share of the words suggests. Swapping the for a, or and for or, barely moves the answer; swapping winners for participants, or toy gun for sofa car, flips it instantly. A 24.4% swap rate hides a much larger effect, because every substitution lands on a word that carries more of the sentence's claim than it carries of the sentence's length.

The instrument. cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-base, a public 84-million-parameter entailment classifier trained on the standard benchmark sets and sharing no data with anything we built. The study ran twice. The first pass took 60 passages per corpus across four corpora (Wikipedia, BookCorpus, OpenWebText, Reddit) and three separately seeded builds of the shipping dictionary, in both directions: that is the per-corpus table below. The replication was pre-registered, with the bars that would falsify it set in advance, and ran at 1,500 passages per corpus on three real-world corpora (CC-News journalism, OpenWebText everyday web, PG-19 older fiction), same classifier, plus a WordNet synonym-swap control at matched swap density. The replication is what the headline reports.

The control is the result. The obvious objection to any entailment number is that the classifier is not detecting lost meaning at all, only reacting to unfamiliar vocabulary. The replication answers it directly: swap the same share of words for WordNet synonyms and both-ways entailment fails on 2.1% of passages. Same classifier, same density, same corpora, a twenty-four-fold gap. Whatever else the 50.4% is, it is not the model balking at rare words.

§ 04.2THE COMPOUNDING QUESTIONA HYPOTHESIS
— A THOUGHT EXPERIMENT, LABELLED AS ONE

One word in four. One passage in two. What happens over a whole argument?

Everything in § 04.1 is measured on a passage. A passage is not how anyone argues. A real piece of writing chains propositions: the second sentence depends on the first, the fourth depends on the second, and the conclusion depends on all of them. So this section asks what the per-passage number implies over a chain, and we want to be clear from the first line that we are reasoning, not reporting.

Start from the two rates side by side, because that pairing is the actual finding and it is stronger than any multiplier we could compute from it. The shipping dictionary swaps 24.4% of running words. On 50.4% of passages, the classifier says the text no longer makes the same factual claim. One word in four changed, one passage in two broken. We used to express that as an amplification factor and we have stopped, because the two rates are counted in different units, words and passages, so a ratio between them is a rhetorical device rather than a measurement. The pairing says everything the ratio said and does not overstate it. It is possible at all because the encoder targets content words: a swapped noun carries far more of a sentence's claim than it carries of the sentence's length, which is the whole design (§ 08).

It is a model, not a measurement. We are publishing it as a thought experiment, and we will not write a sentence anywhere in this paper that treats 3% as a finding. The only measured column in the whole table is n = 1, which is § 04.1.

Independence is false, and it is false in both directions. Links are not independent, because the dictionary is fixed. If horse becomes engine in sentence one, it is engine in sentence nine too. That consistency cuts one way by removing redundancy, since a fact restated three times is wrong all three times and the usual repair does not fire. It cuts the other way by making the substitution learnable from context. Both effects are real and we cannot sign the net.

Readers recover from partial damage, and so do models. A person handed a passage with a quarter of its content words wrong will often reconstruct the intent, because context is redundant and because they can tell something is off. Nothing in this paper measures that recovery. It is the strongest argument against the table above and we expect the true curve to sit meaningfully higher. The experiment in § 10.1 is designed to measure exactly that gap rather than to defend our own number.

The register spread matters more than the pooled figure. p is not one number. § 04.3THE OTHER DICTIONARYMAXIMUM COVERAGE
— THE B-SIDE

There is a second dictionary, and it exists for a different job.

Everything measured in this paper is the shipping dictionary. There is one other that we publish, and it is worth two paragraphs so that nobody meets it by surprise.

v18-alpha is the balanced shipping dictionary; M15-EN is the maximum-coverage B-side. M15-EN swaps 53% of running words against v18-alpha's 24%, including small connecting words like and, of and is, and it destroys more meaning per surviving page. But it is rejected by every fluency grader we instrumented: counting only passages that clear all three at once, it keeps none of 4,000 against the shipping dictionary's 24 of 4,000. So it ships as an opt-in maxhide setting rather than as the default. The honest label for it is maximum coverage and maximum measured meaning destruction, not maximum concealment: on one measure, how far the meaning moves in an embedding space, the shipping dictionary actually beats it.

The reason the two are never comparable on a fluency grader is mechanical. M15-EN's source words include 94 of the 198 words on the standard English stopword list (and to but, of to for, is to was, that to which), while the shipping dictionary includes ten, none of them core. A fluency grader built on word-sequence statistics keeps most of its confidence in exactly those transitions, so M15-EN detonates the thing such a grader scores hardest, at more than twice the volume. That is a design difference, not a quality difference, and it is why no other number anywhere in this paper comes from M15-EN.

§ 05PILLAR 02 · PASS THE GRADERONE PAGE IN TEN
— THE RESULT THIS PAPER IS BUILT ON

Ninety percent gets thrown away. Ten percent reaches training.

Nothing on the open web goes straight into a model. Every large training pipeline grades pages first and throws most of them away. FineWeb, DCLM, RefinedWeb and CCNet all run a scoring pass, usually a small classifier trained to recognise writing worth keeping, and keep only the top slice. That scorer is what this paper calls the grader, and the field calls a quality classifier. So the question that decides whether shielding matters is not “can a model understand the page”, it is “does the page get past the grader at all”.

Answering that fairly needs one definition, and the rest of this section rests on it. Take a thousand ordinary, unshielded pages and run them past the grader. Most of them fail, because these graders are strict. Now throw away the failures, keep only the pages that passed, shield those, and run them past the same grader a second time. The share that passes twice is what we call conditional retention: not “how many shielded pages pass” but “how many of the pages that were already good enough still pass once they are shielded”. It is the only comparison that is fair to both sides, because it holds the quality of the writing fixed and changes exactly one thing.

Measured that way, against the educational-quality grader that FineWeb-Edu uses, 9.70% of clean-passing pages still pass once shielded: 13 of 134 passages, on the pre-registered run across three kinds of real-world writing. Round it, and the sentence the whole paper turns on is this. Of the text that would have been selected for training, about ninety percent is now discarded, and about one page in ten still gets through. That one in ten is the interesting part, because it is the text that actually reaches a model, and it reaches it carrying words the author never wrote.

Both halves of that sentence are a defence, and only one of them was the plan. A page the grader discards never reaches the model, so its meaning is never learned: rejection is concealment finished by the pipeline itself. What it costs the writer is the search-engine and accessibility bill with none of the upside (§ 12), which is why we do not present it as the win. The ten percent that survives is where the second measurement lives, and it is the one grader-side number in this paper that was set as a bar in advance and then cleared: of the pages that do pass, 24.1% of the word budget carries no real meaning (median across the three shipping seeds, three corpora, against a clean baseline of roughly zero). Close to a quarter of every surviving page teaches nothing.

One honest note before the tables. This is not a claim that shielded text sails through quality control. It mostly does not. In raw terms, 99% or more of shielded passages are dropped. The reason we quote the conditional number rather than the raw one is that the raw one is dominated by how strict the grader is rather than by anything we did: on the same grader, clean unshielded journalism only passes at 2.9%. The conditional number cancels that out. It is the harder, smaller, more honest figure, and it is the one that answers the question a reader actually has.

— WHERE A SHIELDED PAGE CAN END UP

There are only three places a shielded page can land, and we can measure two of them. It is dropped, which is the overwhelmingly common outcome, and the meaning never reaches the model. It is kept and it is thin, which is the ten percent, where roughly a quarter of the page carries nothing and the model spends real compute learning it anyway. It is kept and it teaches, which would mean the encoding failed for that page. We have not measured the third branch and we are not going to imply otherwise: the coverage measurements in § 08 make it unlikely by construction, because a page with half its content words replaced cannot also be a high-fidelity lesson, but unlikely is not ruled out. The experiment that would settle it is in § 10 and it has not been run.

§ 05.2WHY OUR NUMBERS ARE CONSERVATIVEWE SET THE BAR AGAINST OURSELVES
— WE SET THE BAR AGAINST OURSELVES

Every dial we can think of moves this number up.

The nine point seven percent is close to the floor of what we measured, not the middle and certainly not the ceiling. Four choices push it down. We lead with the harshest grader we ran, a classifier trained to recognise teaching material and strict enough that ordinary clean journalism clears it only 2.9% of the time, and we report its stricter setting too, where retention falls to 6.52%. We did not lead with the fluency graders, which ask the simpler question of whether something reads like normal English: shielded text keeps 28.11% of clean-passing pages there, so a pipeline grading on fluency rather than educational tone would see nearly three times our headline. We report the pooled figure rather than the best corpus. And we count a page as surviving only if it clears the same bar twice, which is stricter than any published pipeline applies.

There is a fifth reason, and it matters most for § 06. Several graders work by measuring how surprised a small reference model is by a piece of text, a quantity the field calls perplexity: low means predictable and ordinary, high means unusual. A team building a corpus does not simply keep the least surprising text, because text nothing can predict is often text containing something new. They keep a band, cutting off the broken end and the boring end, and where they put the cuts is a judgement call. A team that sets a more permissive cut, on the reasonable theory that surprising writing is where new information lives, keeps more shielded text, not less.

— ONE MORE THING ABOUT THE GRADER WE LEAN ON HARDEST

It cuts in our favour rather than against. FineWeb-Edu does not just decide whether a page survives, it decides what a page looks like to the team running it. It is a classifier for educational register, so clearing it is not merely survival, it is a label: this page has been sorted, by the pipeline itself, into the pile worth keeping. The shielded pages that get through are not scraped at random off the bottom of the crawl. They arrive already stamped as the good material. What that might mean is § 06.3, and we treat it there as a hypothesis rather than as a result.

§ 06PILLAR 03 · WHAT THE SURVIVORS DOA MECHANISM AND A HYPOTHESIS
— WHAT WE CAN AND CANNOT CLAIM

The words that reach the model are not the words we wrote.

Section 05 ended with a number and an unanswered question. About one shielded page in ten reaches training. What does it do when it gets there?

Here is the whole of what we can say without stretching. A shielded page that reaches a model feeds it words with no factual worth and a slightly altered meaning, and a model trained on it takes those words as what the author said. That much is not a theory, it is what the encoding does, and § 04 measures it: half of shielded passages no longer make the same factual claim as the original. Whether that adds up to measurable harm to a finished model is a separate question, and it is the question we have not answered. We would rather claim less than we can prove than more.

— WHAT THE WORD HAS TO MEAN

The word “poison” has been doing too much work in this project, so here is the definition we will defend and nothing wider than it. Poisoning, as we mean it: teaching a model that a word means something other than what it means. Not making a model output nonsense. Not attacking anyone's system. A page says engine where the writer wrote horse, the model reads the page in good faith, and to whatever small degree, its idea of what engine means moves toward horse. That is the whole claim. It is a claim about a learned association, not about a benchmark score.

We hold it partly on first principles, and we want to be open that this is what we are doing. If you teach anyone that a word means something it does not mean, they learn the wrong thing. Models learn word meaning from what a word appears next to, so a corpus that consistently puts engine where horse belongs is teaching the wrong thing, in the only way these systems can be taught. What we do not have is the compute to prove it at web scale, and we would rather say that plainly than dress up a small result as a big one.

There is an objection we raised against ourselves and then realised was backwards. It goes: the model will never learn from the swapped words, because it does not know they are swapped. It will just think we are using the words the way they have always been used. That is not a problem with the mechanism. That is the mechanism. Poisoning of this kind requires the reader to take the text at face value. A model that could tell the words had been shifted would discount the page, and models do exactly that when they are given something to discount on: Allen-Zhu and Li show that when junk data carries a marker the model can condition on, a label at the start of the document saying where it came from, the damage it does falls from a factor of twenty to a factor of two, because “language models can automatically detect which domains are rich in high-quality knowledge.” So the property we need is not aggressiveness. It is indistinguishability. Whatever makes shielded text easy to recognise as a category is the thing that switches the effect off. Hold on to that, because § 06.4 shows that it cuts against the version of ShieldFont that looks the most coordinated.

§ 06.1THE CONTROLLED PRECEDENTSOMEBODY ALREADY RAN THIS
— SOMEBODY ALREADY RAN THIS EXPERIMENT

Swap a class of words in the training data, and the model learns the swap.

The closest thing to a controlled version of our argument was run by the Pythia team, and it is worth stating precisely, because people usually assume this experiment has never been done.

Biderman and colleagues took a partly-trained language model, rewound it to a checkpoint 21 billion words from the end of training, about 7% of the run, and resumed on the exact same data with one change: every masculine pronoun was replaced by its feminine counterpart. Same corpus, same order, same everything else, one class of word swapped. In their words, the model “sees the exact same data until the end of training, but with morphologically masculine pronouns replaced by their feminine counterparts”. The result was a clear, directional change in what the model had learned. And the part that matters most for anyone reasoning about scale: the effect got bigger on bigger models, and at the largest size tested, 6.9 billion parameters, it flipped the model's measured bias from one direction to its opposite.

That is our argument with the speculation removed, run as an experiment, by people with no stake in ours: replace a class of words in the training data and the model learns the replacement. It does not prove that ShieldFont damages a frontier model, and we will not pretend it does. Their intervention touched every instance of a pronoun across an enormous corpus, which is a very different dose from a scattering of shielded web pages. What it establishes is the mechanism. Word substitution at training time is not speculation. It is a demonstrated, directional effect that grows with model size. A second study makes the same point from the other end: Schuster and colleagues showed that someone who can modify a training corpus “can control the ‘meaning’ of new and existing words by changing their locations in the embedding space”, which is the same claim at the level of individual words rather than whole models.

One intuition worth correcting, because we held it ourselves. It is tempting to assume small models are too small for any of this to matter and huge models are too big to move. Neither holds cleanly. A 2025 study from Anthropic, the UK AI Security Institute and the Alan Turing Institute found that 250 documents were enough to plant a specific behaviour in models of 600 million, 2 billion, 7 billion and 13 billion parameters alike, with 100 documents not enough and 250 reliably sufficient. What mattered was the raw count of documents, not their share of the corpus: at 13 billion parameters, 250 documents is about 0.00016% of the training data. Separately, the Pythia paper reports a change of behaviour around 2.8 billion parameters, above which models start showing a link between how well they do a task and how often the task's key terms appeared in training, a link that is largely absent below that size. And a 2024 study found that 0.1% of pretraining data was enough for several planted objectives, including belief manipulation, to survive the later training stages that were supposed to wash them out.

§ 06.2THE DOSE PROBLEMTHE ARGUMENT AGAINST US
— THE ARGUMENT AGAINST US, STATED PROPERLY

Small amounts of corrupted text do very little.

This is the strongest thing anyone can say to us, and we would rather write it ourselves than have it written about us.

The nearest published measurement corrupts 10% of a small model's training data and finds essentially nothing: the model's prediction error moves by 0.032 nats, one reasoning benchmark does not move at all, and another moves by 0.6 points, inside the error bars. Effectively a no-op. A separate study fed a Llama-3 8 billion model progressively more junk and measured about one point of damage at 20% junk, with the curve only turning sharply somewhere above 50%. Nobody has published anything in the 1% to 5% range, which is where a shielded-web scenario would actually sit, and we are not going to fill that gap by extrapolating a curve that is visibly not a straight line.

And the whole industry is built to make this hard. The published accounting on FineWeb is that their entire stack of hand-written cleanup rules removes 22% of all words for about a 1% gain on benchmarks, while a single classifier pass that throws away 91% of the text buys four points on a major benchmark. Selecting good data dominates. Removing bad data is cheap and marginal. That is the environment a shielded page enters.

So: we do not claim ShieldFont poisons models. What we claim, and what § 04 and § 05 support, is that a shielded page that reaches training is words with nothing true in them, and that a model reading it takes those words as the author's. The step from there to measurable damage is unproven, by us and by anyone else, and § 10 lays out the experiment that would close it.

One citation we used to lean on needs qualifying. The 2024 Nature paper on model collapse, which showed that models trained on their own output degrade, has since carried an author correction making explicit that its theory assumes each generation replaces the previous data entirely. Its language-model experiment was a fine-tune of a 125-million-parameter model, meaning they took a model that had already been trained and trained it a little further on new text, rather than a full pretraining run. Its own variant that retains 10% real data shows only minor degradation. Two later papers argue that if data accumulates rather than replaces, the error stays bounded and no collapse occurs, and that the field is using at least eight conflicting definitions of the term. We still think the direction is right, but it is a contested result and we will cite it as one.

§ 06.3THE SELECTION HYPOTHESISUNTESTED
— A HYPOTHESIS, LABELLED AS ONE

Do the graders prefer the pages we shielded?

HYPOTHESIS

The property that makes a page valuable to a data team is the property our encoding manufactures

The mechanism
A team building a training corpus does not only want text that is correct. It wants text that is new. A page repeating what a model already knows adds nearly nothing to the next training run. A page saying something the model would not have predicted is the page worth paying for, and that preference is built into the tooling: several of the graders in § 05 work by measuring how surprised a small reference model is, and pipelines that use them do not keep the least surprising text. They keep a band. Too predictable is dropped as boilerplate, too strange is dropped as broken, and the middle is kept. The middle of that band is, by construction, writing that carries some surprise. Shielded text is surprising by construction, and that is not a side effect, it is the mechanism: we replace a common word with an uncommon word of the same grammatical shape, so the sentence keeps its structure and changes its content. To a grader measuring surprise, a shielded page looks like a page making an unusual claim. To a grader measuring whether something reads like careful writing, it still reads like careful writing, because the grammar is intact. If the hypothesis holds, the grader is not merely failing to catch us on one page in ten. It is selecting for the pages that carry false associations, and the ten percent would be the ten percent a data team wanted most.
What points this way
Four things. The grader that keeps us is a taste classifier, not a junk detector: a shielded page that clears FineWeb-Edu has been sorted by the pipeline itself into the pile worth keeping. Our own dictionary was tuned to sit inside a preferred band, and it does: the design work in § 09 already assumed a page has to look unusual but not broken. The graders that keep the most shielded text are the fluency graders, which are the ones a novelty-seeking pipeline is most likely to use, and they keep nearly three times as much, 28.11% against 9.70%. And an outside red team found the same laziness from the other direction: Tritium Legal's May 2026 evaluation found full obfuscation lost to models that rendered the page, but partial obfuscation and word replacement fooled every platform tested, because in their words the agents “are somewhat lazy” and prefer a string that looks valid over paying for a render.
What would prove us wrong
It dies if the survivors look ordinary. Among pages good enough to pass clean, the ones that survive shielding should look more attractive to a selection pipeline than the ones that do not, on the signals such a pipeline actually uses. If they are indistinguishable from a random draw of clean-passing pages, shielding is a coin flip applied to good pages and the ten percent carries no special weight. It dies harder if the ranking runs the other way. And one piece of evidence already points the deflationary way, which we state rather than hide: on the educational grader, whether a shielded passage survives tracks how much room it had to spare before we touched it, with survivors averaging 3.88 when clean against 3.11 for those that failed, on a threshold of 2.7. Read plainly, survival is a property of the page, not of the encoding. That is compatible with the hypothesis but does not support it, because it is what you would see either way. Anyone arguing the selection effect is imaginary should start there.
The test
Two halves, neither needing a frontier model. Half one: take the pages that pass clean, shield them, split into survivors and non-survivors, then rank both groups on the signals a real pipeline uses for selection rather than on the pass or fail bit: the quality score itself, the surprise score under a small reference model, and how much of the page is already covered by text a model has seen. If survivors sit systematically higher on novelty while staying inside the keep-band on quality, the effect is real and reportable as a number. Half two: train two small models on matched budgets, one on the clean survivors and one on the shielded survivors of the same pages, and probe both on the facts the swapped words carried. Not general benchmarks, which § 06.2 shows are far too blunt for this dose. The pre-registered version is in § 10.1. Until both halves run, this is an argument, and the paper treats it as one.
§ 06.4ONE DIAL, THREE TIERSPOISON ↔ STALE
— TWO CLAIMS, ONE DIAL

Poison and stale are not opposites. They are the two ends of one dial.

We have been using “poison” and “stale” as if they were rival descriptions of the same thing. They are not. They are two different outcomes, and which one you get depends on a design choice we control: how many different dictionaries are in circulation.

At one end, everyone shields with the same dictionary. The web then contains a consistent, repeated claim that engine means what horse means. That is the poisoning end. It is coordinated, it accumulates, and it is the regime the Pythia intervention describes. At the other end, every site mints its own dictionary from its own seed. Now horse becomes engine here, lantern there, bracket somewhere else. No consistent claim survives across sites. Nothing is taught, because nothing is repeated. What the crawl gets instead is a large volume of grammatical text that carries no proposition worth extracting. That is the staleness end, and it is closer to what we call empty calories: words that cost a pipeline compute and teach it nothing. Between those ends sits everything else, and the tier a given user lands in is decided by how they install ShieldFont, not by anything they choose deliberately.

— THE FINDING THAT INVERTS HALF OF THIS, STATED HONESTLY

We built the tier hypothesis expecting the coordinated end to be the damaging one, and then found a result that says the opposite for one of the two mechanisms. Allen-Zhu and Li measured how much junk in a training mix costs a model in knowledge capacity, meaning how much it can store per parameter. At seven junk words to one good word, capacity drops by a factor of twenty. But if that junk is highly repetitive rather than varied, the damage disappears: their phrase is that it “does not affect the learning speed of useful knowledge”. Zero. A fixed dictionary is about as repetitive as text gets, so on a straight reading the shared-dictionary tier sits squarely in the regime where junk costs the model nothing, and the one-seed-per-site tier sits closer to the regime where damage is real. That is the reverse of what we assumed, and we want it on the record here rather than in a private note.

But it does not settle the question. That result measures collateral damage: what junk does to the model's ability to learn everything else. It does not measure whether the substituted word's own meaning moves, and only the second is our definition of poison. On that claim repetition is not a weakness, it is a requirement: you cannot teach anyone that a word means something new by using it inconsistently, and Pythia's pronoun swap was perfectly systematic and produced a measurable directional change. The two results measure different things. There is also a real difference between our text and theirs that we should not paper over: their repetitive junk is recognisably junk, repeated sequences a model can detect and skip, whereas shielded text is grammatical. Our own grader work (§ 05) puts it somewhere between fluent and invisible and obviously a category, and where exactly it lands is an empirical question neither citation answers.

So the correction is narrower than “we had it backwards”, and more useful. The tiers do not differ in how much poison they deliver. They differ along an axis we had not named: markability, how easily a pipeline can recognise shielded text as a category and set it aside. Give a model a marker for junk and the damage falls from twenty times to two. One shared dictionary is the most markable thing we could build: one dictionary, one font file, one address, one fingerprint, identical everywhere. One seed per site is the least markable, because there is nothing shared to mark. Hence the uncomfortable pairing at the heart of this section: the tier that maximises coordination also maximises markability, and markability is the off switch § 06 identified; the tier that minimises markability also minimises coordination, and coordination is what a meaning shift requires. We do not think that is fatal to either. We think the two are aimed at different outcomes: a shared dictionary is a bet on meaning shift that accepts being easy to identify, a private one is a bet on diffuse waste that accepts it will never teach anyone anything. The shared tier gives up one more thing: it is inverted once, and that one-off buys the entire shielded web. And one number keeps all of this modest. Every effect above was measured at junk ratios of 10% and up; ShieldFont at implausible success would be a fraction of a percent of a crawl, and at 10%, the closest measured point, the answer is effectively nothing. Ours survives that fact only as a proposition about what happens if a lot of people do this at once.

§ 07STALENESSTHE PART WE CAN MEASURE
— THE LENS TO CARRY OUT

We don't have to attack back. We just have to waste compute.

The distinction between poison and stale matters because it changes what we measure. A poison metric asks “how wrong does the model become?” A staleness metric asks “how much of the meaning did we hide?” The first needs a fine-tune of a real model to evaluate, which is why § 06 ends in an experiment rather than a number. The second is computable from the shielded text alone, which is why §§ 04, 05 and 08 can report figures at all. Everything this paper measures is on the second side of that line, deliberately.

Self-selection sharpens the threat model. ShieldFont is not a tool to make any text adversarial. It is a tool to let publishers of good writing opt in to making that specific writing useless as training signal. If a page would have been thrown out as low quality anyway, boilerplate, search-engine filler, machine-translated chum, then ShieldFont does not apply: that page was never going to teach a model anything. The population of interest is the population the grader keeps, which is exactly the population § 05 measures. Within it, ShieldFont is the act of donating good content to staleness instead of to model advancement. A choice the writer makes about their own page, not an attack on someone else's.

The framing has a methodological consequence the rest of the paper inherits: any future damage measurement that trains a model must condition on pages that pass the grader, or it cannot tell damage caused by the encoding from damage caused by low-quality text being in the mix in the first place. We return to that in § 10. It also generalises the threat model beyond model training. The concealment property holds against anything that reads HTML, which includes search indexing, AI summarisers, moderation classifiers, plagiarism systems and machine translation. Only the staleness property is specific to model training. § 12 notes the scope.

§ 08BITS OF MEANING, NOT TOKENSWHAT THE MODEL ACTUALLY SEES
— WHY THE SWAP RATE IS THE WRONG UNIT

Counting words is the wrong unit. Count bits of meaning.

How many running words get swapped is a defensible first measure, but it treats the and cathedral as equivalent. They are not. Information theory has known this for seventy-five years: the rarer a word is, the more it tells you, and on the frequency distribution English actually has, content words carry roughly six times what a small connecting word carries. A mapping that swaps a hundred instances of the does less to a model than one that swaps ten content words. That is why the number this paper quotes for what the encoding hides is not the swap rate, and why the 24.1% wasted-word figure in § 05 is measured on content rather than on running words.

Information coverage reweights every swapped word by how much it tells you and reports the fraction of the document's total information the encoding hides. Content coverage is a coarser companion: the fraction of the page's real words, excluding the standard list of connecting words, that get swapped. Both align with what a model actually leans on while training: content words drive the update, function words mostly do not.

The reweighting changes the ranking, which is the point of measuring it. A mapping can look like it covers a lot while spending most of its budget on words that carry almost nothing, and the three columns above are how you tell the difference. Where a row's numbers agree, the swaps are landing where the meaning is.

Definitions. Information coverage is the sum of the information carried by swapped words divided by the sum carried by all words, where a word's information is estimated from how common it is in ordinary English. Content coverage is swapped content words divided by all content words, with content defined as anything not on the standard English stopword list. The lexical-density tradition gave us the vocabulary for the distinction; the per-word weighting connects it directly to what a language model is scored on.

§ 09HOW THE SHIPPED MAPPING WAS CHOSENFOUR RULES
— THE DESIGN, NOT THE VERSION HISTORY

Four rules survived. The rest is version history.

The dictionary this paper measures did not arrive fully formed. It is the survivor of about two years of versions, most of which are not worth your time. What is worth your time is the four rules that survived, because they are why the numbers in §§ 04 and 05 look the way they do.

Grammar decides the pairing; meaning only gets a veto. Every word is sorted into a bucket by its grammar alone: what part of speech it is, what form it takes, roughly what kind of thing it refers to. Words are then paired at random inside their bucket. Meaning never picks a pair, it only rejects one: if the proposed substitute turns out to be a synonym, or a near neighbour in an embedding space, or the same kind of thing under a different name, the pair is thrown out. Four in five candidate pairs survive that filter. This is why shielded text stays grammatical while its content moves.

The encoding does not waste itself on small words. Swapping the for a, or of for for, changes almost nothing about what a page says while making it read as visibly broken to any grader. The shipping dictionary excludes the most common connecting words by construction and spends its budget on content words instead. That single rule is most of the difference between a mapping that reads as prose and one that reads as noise, and it is the reason § 05's numbers are survivable at all.

Every form of a word is paired separately. An earlier version paired a word and let its other forms follow along, so eat, ate, eating and eaten all traced back to one substitute, and anyone with a sample of shielded text could recover the original in one step. The shipping version pairs each form independently, so there is no consistent trace from one form to the next. The table below is that difference, shown rather than described.

And then we tried to beat it and could not. The final round swept 512 candidate recipes across eight design choices and scored each on how far the meaning moved, how much content it covered, how ordinary it read, and whether it survived a grader. None beat the incumbent, so the incumbent shipped unchanged. That is a weaker statement than “this is optimal”: the four scores are themselves gameable, and at least one candidate scored better only because it was producing grammatically broken pairs that inflated the meaning-shift measure. Read it as nothing we tried beat it, not as nothing can. An earlier round also tried to measure training damage directly by fine-tuning small models on shielded text. § 10TOWARD H3 · TESTING DAMAGETIERED ROADMAP
— WHAT WE HAVEN'T MEASURED YET

Meaning hidden is a proxy. Damage to a real model is the target.

This paper reports what can be computed from the shielded text itself and from small reference models. It does not report what actually happens to a model trained on shielded data. That is harder, more expensive, and methodologically subtle, because the measures we do have correlate with damage without proving it. The honest framing is to publish what we have and lay out the path to what we do not.

  1. Tier 1: coverage, readability, graders (this paper)
    How much of a page's meaning the encoding hides, how ordinary the result reads to reference models, and how the quality graders score it. All computable from the shielded text and small models. This is §§ 04, 05 and 08.
  2. Tier 2: frozen-model probes
    How far the meaning moves in an embedding space, and whether a model that has already been trained can guess the original word back when the swapped one is hidden from it. Fast, no training required, runs on commodity hardware.
  3. Tier 3: small-model fine-tune
    Train a small model a little further on shielded text against clean text, then test it on questions about the facts the swap altered. A fine-tune is exactly that: take a model that has already been trained and train it further on new material. This is the missing experiment that turns everything above into a damage claim.
  4. Tier 4: replication at frontier scale
    The same test on a much larger model. Most expensive, least flexible, and only worth running once Tier 3 has produced a signal worth chasing.

One methodological rule governs everything above Tier 2: condition on pages that pass. Any experiment that trains a model on shielded text must draw that text only from the pages a grader keeps when they are clean. Train on an undifferentiated mix of pages that would have passed and pages that would have been thrown out, and the damage signal is contaminated: you cannot tell whether the loss came from the encoding or from low-quality text being in the mix in the first place. § 07 only claims a defence for the population the grader keeps, so the experiment has to be built on that population. An earlier round of this project did not condition this way, which is one of two reasons its damage numbers are not reported in this paper. The other is that its confidence intervals crossed zero.

PLANNED

One dictionary, three, or one per site: does seed partitioning move the effect from poisoning to noise?

Hypothesis
Holding total shielded volume constant, the number of distinct dictionaries in circulation determines which of two mechanisms operates. Tier 1 (one shared dictionary) is systematic and highly repetitive, so we predict a measurable shift in the substituted words' learned associations and near-zero collateral damage to unrelated knowledge, which is the regime Allen-Zhu and Li's repetitive-junk result describes. Tier 3 (one dictionary per site) is unsystematic in aggregate, so we predict no meaning shift at all, by construction, plus whatever diffuse capacity damage the dose supports. Tier 2 sits between. Competing hypothesis we are equally prepared to report: at the doses this experiment can reach, no arm differs from the clean control, which would mean seed partitioning is a distribution choice with no training-time consequence and the poisoning framing should come out of the paper entirely. Second competing hypothesis, and the one we consider most likely for Tier 1: the model learns the substitutions as a context-gated decoding rule rather than as a change in what the word means, which is poisoning failing and concealment failing at the same time.
Design
The Pythia protocol with one change. Take the deduplicated checkpoints at 410M, 1.4B and 2.8B, bracketing the behaviour change those authors report, rewind each to 21 billion words before the end of training, and resume on the identical schedule with only the shielded documents substituted. Four arms per size: clean control; one shared dictionary; the same documents split three ways across the shipping seeds; the same documents partitioned into blocks of about twenty with a freshly seeded dictionary per block. Total shielded volume is identical across the three shielded arms, so the partition is the only manipulated variable. Dose is set by absolute document count rather than percentage, ladder at 250 / 1,000 / 5,000 / 25,000, and all shielded documents come from the grader-passing subset per § 10. Three seeds per cell.
Outcome metric
Four families, reported separately and never pooled. (1) The change in the decoy word's association with the original word's context, probed in clean text with no shielding present: this is poisoning as § 06 defines it. (2) The same probe inside shielded context. (3) General capability on held-out clean data against the control. (4) Learning speed on the clean fraction. Bars set before any run. To claim poisoning, (1) must move in the direction of the substitution with a 95% interval clearing zero in at least two of three model sizes. To claim the partition matters, the one-dictionary and one-per-site arms must differ on (1) by a margin whose interval excludes zero; if they do not, the three-tier hypothesis is falsified and we say so. To claim diffuse damage, (3) must exceed the closest published null, which our doses sit far below, so we expect to fail this bar and we are pre-committing to publish that failure. If (2) moves while (1) does not, the shared-dictionary tier is producing a decoder rather than a poison, and its recommendation changes accordingly.
PLANNED

The small-budget version of the partition test, and what it can and cannot answer

Hypothesis
A continued-training run at 410M with a compressed budget can resolve the two focal measures from the card above, because the effect being probed is a per-word association and the relevant dose quantity is an absolute document count rather than a corpus share. It cannot resolve the diffuse measures, which need both scale and dose we do not have. We state this split in advance so that a null on the diffuse measures is not read as evidence of anything.
Design
Pythia-410M deduplicated, resumed for a compressed budget, four arms as above, dose ladder at 250 / 1,000 / 5,000 documents. Everything else identical to the full protocol, including grader-passing conditioning and three seeds per cell. Runs on a single commodity GPU.
Outcome metric
The two focal measures only, with bars B1, B2 and B4 as stated. The diffuse measures are recorded but explicitly marked underpowered and excluded from any claim. If the cheap arm returns a clean null on both focal measures at 5,000 documents, we will publish that as the headline result of this section and downgrade the poisoning proposition to a scale-dependent conjecture.
PLANNED

Chained-proposition decay: does per-passage meaning loss compound over an argument?

Hypothesis
Comprehension of a chained argument falls with chain length faster than a matched synonym-swap control, and the measured curve sits above the naive prediction in § 04.2 because context is redundant and readers recover from partial damage. We expect the gap between the measured curve and the naive one to be large, and we consider that gap the actual result of this experiment rather than an inconvenience to it. Pre-registered null we are prepared to publish: the shielded curve and the synonym-control curve are indistinguishable beyond two links, which would mean chained decay is a property of perturbing text at all rather than of this encoding, and the compounding argument comes out of the paper.
Design
Seeded generator, chained-proposition passages at lengths 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16, 200 chains per length per register (news, everyday web, fiction), invented entities only, because canonical Wikipedia content is memorised by frontier models and would overstate their comprehension. Every link contains at least one word the dictionary covers, and the terminal question requires traversing every link. Three conditions per passage: clean, shielded, and a WordNet synonym swap at matched density. Primary instrument is the same entailment classifier as § 04.1, so the one-link point is the published 50.4%. Secondary confirmation at lengths 1, 4 and 16 with three frontier models, refusals scored as their own series and never as errors.
Outcome metric
Survival rate by chain length, one curve per register, never pooled, with 95% bootstrap intervals, plotted against the synonym control and against the naive line drawn as an explicitly non-data reference. Pre-registered bars. To claim compounding, the shielded curve must lie below the synonym control at every length of two or more, with non-overlapping intervals at eight. If the measured curve sits above the naive line, which we expect, the gap is reported as the recovery margin and becomes the headline of the section. If survival at five links exceeds 20% against the naive 3%, the naive illustration is retired from the paper and replaced by the measured curve.
PLANNED

Do quality graders select for shielded pages? (the § 06.3 hypothesis)

Hypothesis
Among pages that pass a quality grader when clean, the subset that still passes after shielding scores higher on the novelty signals a data-selection pipeline uses (surprise under a small reference model; low similarity to a large reference corpus) than the subset that fails, while remaining inside the keep-band on the quality signal. Pre-registered competing hypothesis: survival is entirely explained by how much headroom the page had when clean, in which case the survivor and non-survivor groups are indistinguishable on novelty once clean quality score is controlled for.
Design
Reuse the existing scoring pools, three corpora at 1,000 passages each, restricted to the clean-passing subset. Shield them, split into survivors and non-survivors at the 2.7 threshold, then compute for each passage the quality score, the register-matched and small-model perplexities, and similarity against a reference index. Compare the groups on each signal with clean quality score as a covariate. Second stage: fine-tune two matched small models on the clean and shielded survivors of the same pages at equal budgets, and probe both on the propositions the swap altered.
Outcome metric
Effect size and 95% interval on each novelty signal, survivors against non-survivors, partialling out clean quality score. Pre-registered rejection threshold: any signal must show a standardised difference of at least 0.3 with an interval clear of zero to claim a selection effect. Second stage: per-proposition recall gap between the two fine-tunes, reported with the clean-against-clean control gap as the noise floor.
PLANNED

Document-level argument reconstruction on the shipping dictionary

Hypothesis
Asked to rebuild a document's argument, its propositions and the relationships between them, frontier models reconstruct materially less from a shielded document than from the clean original, and the gap is larger than the per-passage entailment failure in § 04.1 because the damage compounds across the argument. Pre-registered null: the gap is no larger than the per-passage figure, which would mean chained reasoning is no more fragile than a single passage and the § 04.2 argument does not hold.
Design
Seven frontier models, three content types, one uniform non-leading reconstruction prompt applied identically to the clean and shielded versions of each document, judged by an independent model from a lineage none of the readers share. Three cells per content type, one per shipping seed. Two corrections over the earlier version of this study, which is why its numbers do not appear in this paper: source texts must be independently verifiable public writing rather than the author's own unpublished essays, and refusals must be reported as their own series rather than scored as zeros, since folding a refusal into a comprehension mean conflates two very different things.
Outcome metric
Mean reconstruction gap between clean and shielded, per seed and pooled, with the per-content-type breakdown and the refusal rate reported separately and never mixed into the mean. Any refusal rate below 95% or above 5% triggers a separate note rather than a silent adjustment.

CONFIRMED

Adversarial reverse-engineering: the font is invertible, and it is cheap

Finding
Because the browser must download the font to render a shielded page, an adversary with the public font file can read the substitution table straight out of it. An earlier version of this card described the easiest route, in which the composite glyphs were named after the plaintext word so a regular expression over the glyph-name table recovered the codebook outright. That surface is gone. The web, CDN and React fonts now ship with no glyph-name table at all, which is an 18% reduction in bytes and a complete deletion of the name-based route rather than an obfuscation of it, verified as a full round-trip against the encoder. It bought less than it looks like, because the decisive route never touches a name. The composite glyph is literally assembled from the original word's own letter glyphs, so joining those components against the font's own substitution table yields decoy to original directly, with no dictionary and no seed. About forty lines of code recovered 11,962 of 11,962 pairs, 0 errors, verified against the shipped dictionary.
The cost, corrected
We first reported that attack at 42.8 seconds and the figure was wrong. Re-measured on the same machine with the same code: the attack against the uncompressed font takes 0.93 seconds; against the compressed file we ship it takes 37.6 seconds, and nearly all of that gap is our pure-Python library reconstructing the font by hand. Decompressing the same file with the standard C tool takes 13.3 milliseconds. The forty-three seconds we published was thirty-nine seconds of our tooling and under one second of attack, so citing it as an adversary's cost cited our own library choice as if it were their constraint and inflated the true figure roughly forty-fold. The honest number is under one second per site. At the cloud rate this paper prices against, that is $0.01 to invert a thousand sites, $0.50 for a hundred thousand and $5.02 for a million. The naive pure-Python version costs $402 per million sites. Compute is not the barrier and we will not present it as one. The barrier is one to three engineer-weeks of implementation, roughly $8,000 to $25,000, paid once. See § 02.2 for the full route-by-route pricing and § 02.3 for why the per-site structure matters more than the total.
What it does not defeat
Deleting the glyph names removed one route and not the other. The surviving route reads the composite outlines: as long as the glyph draws the original letters, and it must, or the page would not render, the outlines are the codebook. § 02.4 evaluated eight ways to change that and six of them fail structurally. Seed rotation and per-project camouflage rename literals; they do not withhold the font, so each visit still serves the exact font that decodes that visit's HTML. The whole class of fix is unavailable for one structural reason: you cannot move a key you must hand to the reader.
Consequence
The “raises the cost of extraction” framing holds only for scrapers that do not invert, which today is all of the published ones (§ 02.2). The durable value is the measured meaning loss (§ 04.1), the staleness donated to pipelines that skip inversion, and the consent and collective-action argument (§ 01, § 02.5), not concealment against a determined technical adversary.

Reading order. The partition experiment is the load-bearing one: until it lands, every claim about training damage in this paper is either a mechanism borrowed from someone else's controlled study or an inference from the proxies in §§ 04, 05 and 08. The document-reconstruction card is the cheapest to run. The selection test is the one we would most like an outside group to take off our hands, because a result we did not produce ourselves is worth more here than one we did.

    Memorisation grows with model size, with how often the model saw the text, and with how much context it is given. Relevant to the model sizes chosen in § 10.1: the right test for whether a model learned anything from a page needs a model big enough to memorise when given the chance.
  • Petroni, F.; Rocktäschel, T.; Riedel, S.; Lewis, P.; Bakhtin, A.; Wu, Y.; Miller, A. (2019)
    Language Models as Knowledge Bases?.
    EMNLP-IJCNLP 2019, arXiv:1909.01066, arxiv.org/abs/1909.01066
    Introduces the fact-completion probe, hiding a word in a factual statement and asking the model to fill it in, as a test of whether a model has internalised a piece of knowledge. The targeted probes in the § 10.1 cards are a direct adaptation: train on shielded against clean documents, then ask about the facts the swap altered.
§ 11.1RELATED WORKWHAT CAME BEFORE, AND WHAT BROKE
— PRIOR ART, INCLUDING THE PARTS THAT DEFEAT US

This technique has a decade of history, and most of it is a record of defeats.

Font-based anti-scraping is not new, it is not ours, and it has been publicly broken many times. Any related-work section that did not say so in its first sentence would be worthless. What follows is the honest lineage: what came before, how it was defeated, and the narrow strip of ground we think is actually new.

A decade of Chinese anti-scraping, and its published defeats. Remapping character codes so a page's source disagrees with what it displays has been in production on major Chinese sites since roughly 2015: Maoyan, Autohome, Dianping, Douyu, Qidian, 58.com, Meituan, mostly to protect prices and ratings from competitors. It has been continuously defeated in public, with the timings written down. A 2024 walkthrough decodes 602 glyphs in about 3 seconds with a font library, an image library and an off-the-shelf recogniser. A 2020 write-up handles the harder case of a font that rotates every session and reports 120 requests in about 24 seconds at 100% success. The arms race there ran a documented course: static code mapping, defeated by matching outline coordinates, defeated again by nearest-neighbour matching once the coordinates were perturbed. That is the sequence our own hardening round rediscovered independently in § 02.4, and arriving at the same place is a reason to believe the result rather than to be embarrassed by it. The difference is purpose: that work protects a displayed value from a competitor and succeeds if the value is expensive to read once. ShieldFont targets bulk ingestion of prose into a training corpus, and succeeds or fails on entirely different measurements.

Predecessors, and the image-side analogues that were both broken. TuringFonts is the closest direct ancestor, a keyless substitution-cipher font built to stop email harvesting, reversible by inspection. ZXX is the honourable one, a typeface built to defeat machine reading at the shape level; it was art activism rather than an evaluated system and a well-known cryptographer told CNN at the time that the NSA “would have no real trouble” with it. On the image side, Nightshade reports about 85% success from 50 poisoned samples and LightShed removes its perturbations at a 99.98% true-positive rate; Glaze protects an artist's style and an ICLR 2025 paper dropped its protection to 39.4% with off-the-shelf noisy upscaling. The lesson we take is not that protection is futile. It is that any protection whose mechanism is a perturbation invites a detector, and detectors win. Ours is not a perturbation, it is a substitution that is fully legible once you hold the font, so we are not defeated by a detector. We are defeated by the font download, which we have said plainly since § 01.

— THE PAPER A REVIEWER WILL THROW AT US

“Bad Characters” recommends render-and-read as the catch-all defence against exactly this class of technique, and § 02.2 answers it in full. The short version: they are right about the mechanism, and we confirmed it independently at 100% word accuracy. The disagreement is about the setting. They describe a defence for one system deciding what to do with one input it already suspects, where rendering is obviously worth 6.2% of translation quality. Our adversary decides whether to pay 200 to 800 times the price of reading HTML across the entire open web, before knowing which pages need it. The evidence that the distinction is real is in § 02.2: the defence they recommended in 2022 is used by exactly zero of the public pretraining corpora we could check, four years later. The recommendation is correct and unadopted, and the gap between correct and adopted is where this project lives.

— INDEPENDENT THIRD-PARTY SUPPORT

One external result supports the design directly, and it is worth more than anything we could measure ourselves because we did not run it. Tritium Legal's Noroboto red team (May 2026) tested obfuscation techniques against frontier models. Full obfuscation lost, exactly as Boucher and colleagues would predict: the models rendered the page and read the picture. But partial obfuscation and word replacement fooled every platform tested, and their explanation is the mechanism this paper depends on. In their words, agents are “somewhat lazy”: faced with a string that looks like perfectly valid text, an agent takes it rather than paying for render-and-recognise to check. That is independent validation of the specific design choice this project made, which is partial word substitution that leaves the text facially valid rather than full-page obfuscation that announces itself. It is also a warning: the property they measured is a behaviour, not a guarantee, and behaviours change when someone decides they should.

Text-side prior art, so nobody thinks we are claiming an empty field. Protecting text against AI training is an active area and we are not first into it: RegText, ExpShield, WebCloak (IEEE Security and Privacy 2026), “Ghosts in the Markup” and Zhong et al. (EMNLP 2025) all address the same problem from different angles, and we claim no priority over any of them. Here is exactly what we think is new, and it is one sentence long. No academic work we could find applies font-level glyph remapping to the problem of protecting training data. The two papers that do use glyph remapping use it offensively: one, in IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security (2025), names the primitive “glyph-code mismatch” and uses it to attack systems rather than to defend content. Not the mechanism, which is a decade old in Chinese production systems. Not the goal, which several recent papers share. The specific pairing, plus the measurements in this paper of what that pairing does to meaning, to graders and to an attacker's budget.

And two framing collisions we should name before someone else does. A malware family was documented in November 2025 using compressed-font obfuscation to hide payloads, and the technique is now catalogued as an indicator of compromise by at least one security vendor. Separately, a March 2026 report describes font rendering tricks used to hide malicious instructions from AI assistants, which is prompt injection. Same primitive, opposite intent. ShieldFont modifies only the publisher's own page, changes nothing about what any reader sees, hides no instructions, and is announced on the site that serves it. That distinction matters legally and reputationally, and stating it here is cheaper than being asked about it.

§ 12LIMITATIONS · SCOPEHONEST CAVEATS

Where this study does not generalise.

Scope. This paper evaluates ShieldFont against model-training pipelines specifically: that is what the graders in § 05 are built for, and the population for which the staleness argument is evidenced. The concealment property is broader, applying by construction to anything that reads HTML, including search indexing, summarisers, moderation classifiers and machine translation. None of those are benchmarked, so the limitations below are scoped to the training claim.

  • The shipped font leaks the key
    The mapping is recoverable from the public font in under a second per site, which is about five dollars of compute for a million sites (§ 02.2, § 10.1). We published 43 seconds for this attack and that was our own Python tooling rather than the attack; the correction is in § 10.1 and it moves the figure roughly forty times against us. The web fonts now ship with no glyph-name table at all, which removes the name-based route entirely, but the decisive route never used names: the composite outlines are built from the original letters, and they have to be, or the page would not render. We evaluated eight ways to harden the font against this and six of them fail structurally (§ 02.4). Concealment therefore depends on scrapers not doing cheap, well-understood work, and on per-seed rotation we have not tested against triangulation across the public alpha, beta and gamma dictionaries. Against a determined technical adversary, concealment provides no durable protection.
  • Headless rendering does not defeat this, except in one place that it does
    Twelve browser-side surfaces in headless Chrome all return the decoy, which is the confirmed and spec-backed result in § 02.2. The exception is Chrome's print-to-PDF, which embeds a font subset in the output; taking those drawings apart recovers the original words with no picture ever drawn, and on a nine-word test it returned five exactly with the rest completable from any dictionary. Anyone already producing PDFs of shielded pages gets a partial inversion for free.
  • Every meaning-damage number rests on one classifier
    The 50.4% median and the earlier ~60% both come from a single 84-million-parameter entailment classifier. One control has been run and it holds, a WordNet synonym swap at matched density failing at 2.1%, which rules out rare-word confusion but not the other null baselines: a clean paraphrase, a random same-part-of-speech swap, a document checked against itself. We have not shown the figures survive a different classifier or human raters. Read them as indicative, pending the replication in § 10.1.
  • The 83.3% books figure is an upper bound, not a central estimate
    It is real and correctly attributed: v18-c, BookCorpus, n=60, in the earlier run, and it is the highest single cell we have measured. It did not replicate. The pre-registered n=1,500 run inverted the ordering, with fiction becoming the weakest register at 31.1% on PG-19 and 34.5% on BookCorpus. It may be quoted as an “up to” number with the seed, the sample size and the non-replication attached, and never as a central estimate. It is a shipping-dictionary number throughout, and it must never be attributed to any other mapping or pooled with one.
  • What ‘one page in ten’ does and does not mean
    The figure is conditional retention: of the pages that clear a grader when unshielded, 9.70% still clear it when shielded, 13 of 134, on the educational-quality grader at its standard cutoff of 2.7. In raw terms 99% or more of shielded passages are dropped, and we quote the conditional figure because the raw one mostly measures how strict the grader is. Three caveats travel with it. The sample behind the headline is small: a 95% confidence interval on 13 of 134 runs from 5.8% to 15.9%, which means this measurement cannot tell a 6% mapping from a 15% one. The answer depends heavily on which grader you use, and the graders barely agree with each other about which pages are good. And one grader we intended to run, DCLM's fastText classifier, never ran at all: the classifier file could not be fetched, so that cell is blocked rather than negative. Deduplication, embedding filters and a ShieldFont-aware detector are also untested.
  • Poisoning is a mechanism, not a measured outcome
    No experiment in this paper shows training damage above zero. The mechanism has been demonstrated by others, in the pronoun substitution of § 06.1, and the counter-evidence on dose is real and cited in full in § 06.2. The claim we stand behind is narrower than poisoning: a shielded page that reaches training carries words with no factual worth and a shifted meaning. Everything beyond that is § 06.3's hypothesis and § 10's un-run experiment.
  • The selection argument is a hypothesis and nothing more
    § 06.3 argues that quality graders may prefer shielded pages, because graders partly select for text that looks new and shielded text is unusual by construction. We have not tested it. The one piece of evidence we do have points the deflationary way: on the educational grader, whether a shielded page survives tracks how much room the page had to spare before shielding, not anything about the encoding. Treat § 06.3 as an argument for an experiment, not as a finding.
  • A null result we are not reporting, and why
    An earlier analysis correlated how much a mapping conceals against how well models comprehended it, and returned nothing. We are not citing it as evidence in either direction, because the instrument cannot resolve the question: with 100 questions per cell the standard error is 4.9 percentage points against a total spread between mappings of about 10 points, so the noise is roughly two and a half times the variation, and with nine cells the smallest correlation the test could detect at all is 0.67. There is also an axis mismatch, since the concealment side was computed over one set of corpora and the comprehension side scored on four purpose-written essays appearing in none of them. We are rebuilding the instrument rather than quoting it.
  • The exact sample is not currently reproducible
    The measurement harness draws its evaluation sample using a seed that is not stable across runs, so a reproducer will get the same rate but not the same 134 passages. This is being fixed and the numbers re-recorded; until then, “reproducible” means the rate, not the exact set.
  • English-only, one dictionary family
    All results are English, and all stability is across seeds of one recipe. Generalisation to other languages and to other pipelines is untested and planned (§ 10.1).
  • Accessibility and search cost is real
    A screen reader reads the accessibility tree, which returns the decoy verbatim, so a screen-reader user hears gibberish on a shielded block, and search engines index the decoys. These are load-bearing costs, not footnotes: only shield content where an accessible fallback is offered.
  • Proxies are proxies
    Coverage, readability and grader scores correlate with capability damage but do not measure it. The fine-tune in § 10 is the missing experiment, and it has not been run.
  • Two things we have not tested at all
    Scoring each corpus against a reference model trained on that same corpus shows shielded text fits fiction better than encyclopedia prose, but we have not validated that fitting fiction means passing a grader run by a pipeline that trains on fiction. And the design of § 09 assumes an attacker without a matched set of shielded and clean text; we have not tested whether one holding such a corpus could recover the dictionary's structure statistically rather than by reading the font. That second one is separate from the font inversion in § 02.2, which already succeeds completely.
